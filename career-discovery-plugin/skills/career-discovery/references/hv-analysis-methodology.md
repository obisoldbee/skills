# HV Career Analysis Methodology

## Purpose

Use two complementary axes to understand a career, then synthesize them into a defensible positioning hypothesis.

- **Vertical / longitudinal**: how the person's work and capabilities evolved over time.
- **Horizontal / cross-sectional**: how responsibilities, capabilities, and outcomes compare across projects, roles, and target-role expectations at a shared frame.
- **Cross-axis synthesis**: what the combination reveals that either axis alone would miss.

## Vertical analysis

Build a timeline with:
- period and role;
- business and organizational context;
- responsibility and decision scope;
- representative projects;
- changes in complexity or autonomy;
- turning points and transitions;
- capability gained or strengthened;
- evidence and unresolved dates.

Look for path dependencies:
- Which early experiences shaped later choices?
- Which decisions expanded the user's scope?
- Which setbacks changed the user's working method?
- Which capabilities recur across different environments?

Do not force a growth narrative if the evidence shows lateral movement, specialization, interruption, or a change of direction.

## Horizontal analysis

Choose a comparison frame that matches the question:

1. **Across projects**: compare the user's role, decisions, complexity, and outcomes across projects in the same period.
2. **Across responsibilities**: compare formal job expectations with actual work.
3. **Across capabilities**: compare the strength and evidence for different capability areas.
4. **Against target roles**: map a job description to confirmed evidence.
5. **Against external role context**: use current, sourced job descriptions or reliable role frameworks when the user wants market positioning.

For every comparison, state the frame, date, and source. Distinguish:
- direct evidence;
- user-reported comparison;
- external source;
- analyst hypothesis.

Avoid unsupported claims such as "top 1%", "rare talent", "far above peers", or "industry-leading".

## Cross-axis synthesis

Ask:
- Which current strength has the clearest historical origin?
- Which capability appears in multiple projects despite changing context?
- Which combination of skills is supported by evidence?
- Which role requirements are supported by a sequence of experiences?
- Which career transition is best explained by the user's actual work?
- What is the strongest positioning hypothesis?
- What evidence would falsify that hypothesis?

## Outputs

Produce, when useful:
- career timeline;
- project comparison matrix;
- capability matrix;
- target-role evidence map;
- positioning hypotheses;
- gaps and confidence notes.

The analysis is a tool for discovery, not a verdict about the user's worth or market rank.
